This is Thomas Schelling’s model of segregation. Each square is content when enough of its neighbours share its colour; outlined squares are not, and move. Set how much alike company each square wants, press play, and watch mild preferences add up to a sorted board. Pause at any point to move squares yourself.

How the model works

The rule. A square’s neighbourhood is the 8 cells around it, or the 24 within two steps in Wide view. Its alike share is s = alike neighbours ÷ occupied neighbours. It is content when s is at least the threshold t you set; in Prefers mixing it must also be at most 75%. A square with no neighbours is content.

A round. Moving takes time, so in each round a discontented square moves with probability 30%. A square that moves looks at 6 empty cells chosen at random and takes the nearest one where it would be content; if none of them suits, it stays where it is. Separately, 1% of all squares move to a random empty cell each round for reasons of their own, as people do, so the board never quite freezes and some squares are always unsettled. The run stops once alike neighbours change by less than one point over 20 rounds, or after 150 rounds.

The measures. Alike neighbours is the average of s over all squares that have neighbours. If mixed at random is the value expected if the same squares were shuffled: Σg ng(ng − 1) / N(N − 1). That is 50% for two equal groups, about 17% for six, and about 62% for a 75:25 split. Content is the share of squares that are content right now. Changing the threshold replays the same starting board, so runs can be compared.

What to look for. Schelling’s point is the gap between the dashed line and the orange one. With a threshold of 35%, no square minds being in the minority, yet the board typically levels off well above the random mix. Because a few squares keep moving for other reasons, content never quite reaches 100%, and alike patches keep slowly growing even once nearly everyone is content. Set the threshold to 75% and the board stays frustrated: many squares can never be satisfied. With a 75:25 split, the majority’s alike share barely moves from its 75% starting point, while the minority’s rises far above its 25% share of the population; some minority squares may never find a place that suits them. In Prefers mixing, squares that dislike being a large majority still end up well above the random mix and often never settle, in line with Zhang (2004) and Pancs and Vriend (2007).

This is Mark Granovetter’s threshold model of collective action. Each of fifty people will join a protest once enough others already have; the number on each square is how many it needs. How the model works

The rule. Each of 50 people has a threshold t: the number of others who must already be protesting before they join. In each round, everyone whose threshold is met by the crowd at the start of the round joins at once. Nobody leaves. The run ends when a round adds no one. A threshold of 0 is an instigator, who protests alone.

The chart. The grey steps count how many people have a threshold of x or less; call that F(x). If x people are out, F(x) will be out next round, so the crowd climbs the orange staircase between the steps and the dashed line y = x. It stops at the first place the steps touch the line, where exactly as many people are out as are willing to be.

What to look for. Granovetter’s point is that the same average preferences can produce opposite outcomes. In Riot, thresholds run 0, 1, 2 … 49 and the whole square joins. Change one person’s threshold from 1 to 2 (One missing) and the protest dies after the instigator, though the two crowds are almost identical. Watching the outcome, an observer would call the first crowd radical and the second moderate. In Bell curve, hold the average fixed and widen the spread: a little more variety in thresholds can supply the early joiners a cascade needs, and the crowd flips from nobody to nearly everyone.

The people. The ages, jobs and circumstances are invented for colour and don’t affect the model; only the threshold does.
